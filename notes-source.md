# Dovi's LSAT Study Notes

*Copy-by-hand notes covering every Logical Reasoning and Reading Comprehension question type, plus conditional logic, flaws, traps, timing and review.*

*Revised 27 Oct 2026.*

## N1. Basics + abbreviations
- P = premise: a stated fact or reason offered as support. C = conclusion: the main claim the argument tries to prove. SC = sub-conclusion (defined below). A = assumption: an unstated idea the argument needs to get from P to C; it fills the gap.
- Question-type shorthand: NA = necessary assumption. SA = sufficient assumption. MBT = must be true. MSS = most strongly supported. MBF = must be false (cannot be true). PSA = principle-strengthen: pick the principle that most helps justify the argument. RC = Reading Comprehension. BR = blind review: redoing questions untimed before checking answers (see N10).
- Symbol shorthand: ~ means "not" (~A = not A). → means "if-then" (A → B = if A, then B). Full rules in N2.
- Conclusion indicator words (what follows is usually the C): therefore, thus, hence, so, consequently, accordingly, it follows that, clearly, shows that. Example: "Sales fell, so the ad failed" (C = the ad failed).
- Premise indicator words (what follows is usually a P): because, since, for, given that, as, after all, in that, for example. Example: "The ad failed, since sales fell" (P = sales fell).
- Sub-conclusion (SC): a claim that is supported by a premise AND itself supports the main conclusion. Flow: P → SC → C. Example: "Ann trained daily (P), so she is fit (SC), so she will finish the race (C)."
- Counterpoint: an opposing view the author mentions only to reject it, flagged by "some argue", "critics claim", "it is often thought". The author's own C usually comes right after a pivot word (but, however, yet, nevertheless, although). Example: "Some say coffee is harmful. However, ..."
- Background: context that sets the scene only; it is neither a premise nor the conclusion and supports nothing.
- Finding the C, "therefore" test: put "therefore" in front of the candidate sentence; if the rest of the argument reads as support for it, it's the C.
- Finding the C, "why?" test: ask "why should I believe this?" about the candidate; if another sentence answers, it's the C. A premise is a sentence that nothing in the argument backs up.
- Universal method for every LR (Logical Reasoning) question: read the question stem first → find the C → find the P → find the gap (what the P doesn't prove about the C) → pre-phrase (predict in your own words) the answer → only then read the choices.

## N2. Conditional logic
- How to read the symbols: A → B means "if A, then B." ~A means "not A."
- In A → B, A is the sufficient condition: if A happens, B is guaranteed. B is the necessary condition: A can't happen without B. Example: "If it's a dog, it's a mammal" (dog → mammal).
- Contrapositive (always valid): flip the order AND negate both sides. A → B becomes ~B → ~A. Example: not a mammal → not a dog.
- Two classic flaws: flipping without negating (B → A: "it's a mammal, so it's a dog") and negating without flipping (~A → ~B: "not a dog, so not a mammal"). Both are wrong.
- Words that introduce the sufficient (put it on the left of the arrow): if, when, whenever, any, each, every, all, the only.
- Words that introduce the necessary (put it on the right of the arrow): only if, only, only when, requires, needs, must, depends on, essential, prerequisite.
- "A only if B" = A → B. "Only A are B" = B → A (the word "only" points at the necessary). "The only A are B" = A → B (adding "the" flips it).
- Unless / without / until / except all mean "if not." Easiest method: whatever follows "unless" becomes the necessary, and you negate the other part to make the sufficient. "A unless B" = ~A → B. Example: "I'll fail unless I study" = not fail → study (if I passed, I studied). Same rule written as its contrapositive: not study → fail.
- "No A are B" = A → ~B (if it's A, it's not B). Its contrapositive is B → ~A.
- "A if and only if B" = A ↔ B: the arrow goes both ways, so A and B always happen together or not at all.
- Chaining: if A → B and B → C, then A → C. Contrapositive of the chain: ~C → ~A.
- To link two rules, find a term they share. If the shared term doesn't line up, take the contrapositive of one rule so it does.
- Contrapositive with "and" / "or": negate each term AND switch "and" to "or" (or "or" to "and"). A → B and C becomes ~B or ~C → ~A. A or B → C becomes ~C → ~A and ~B.
- You can split "and" on the right side: A → B and C means A → B, and also A → C. You can split "or" on the left side: A or B → C means A → C, and also B → C. Never split "and" on the left or "or" on the right.
- Negating a group: not (A and B) = not A, or not B (at least one fails). Not (A or B) = not A and not B (both fail).
- Two rules with the same left side (A → B, A → C): A guarantees both B and C. Two rules with the same right side (A → C, B → C): you learn nothing about how A and B relate. That's a common trap answer.
- "All A are B" is a conditional (A → B). "Most" and "some" are not conditionals, so you can't chain them the same way (see N3).

## N3. Quantifiers + numbers
- Quantifier ladder, strongest to weakest: all > most (more than half) > some (at least one, and it could be all) > none. "Many" and "several" only mean "some". Example: "some students passed" is still true if every student passed.
- "Some" reverses: "some A are B" = "some B are A" (some doctors are runners = some runners are doctors). "Most" does NOT reverse: most doctors are runners doesn't mean most runners are doctors.
- Negating quantifiers: not all = at least some are not. Not some = none. Not most = half or fewer. Not none = at least some. Not always = sometimes not. Example: the opposite of "all cats nap" is "some cats don't nap", not "no cats nap".
- Valid: all A are B (A → B) + some C are A ⇒ some C are B. Example: all dogs are mammals + some pets are dogs ⇒ some pets are mammals.
- Valid: most A are B + all B are C ⇒ most A are C. Example: most students are athletes + all athletes are insured ⇒ most students are insured.
- Valid: most A are B + most A are C ⇒ some B are C, because two majorities of the same group must overlap. Example: most of the class plays chess + most plays piano ⇒ some chess players play piano.
- Invalid, nothing follows: some + some; some + most; most + most when the shared term isn't the subject of both; "most A are B + most B are C" (the B's that are C may not be the A's).
- Percent is not number: a rate can rise while the count falls if the base shrinks, and a bigger share is not automatically a bigger amount. Example: crime rate up 10% but the town shrank, so fewer actual crimes.
- More X in total is not more X per person (a big country can have more doctors but fewer per person). A big percent increase from a tiny base can still be a small number (1 to 3 is +200%).
- "Average" hides spread: a few extreme values can pull it up or down, so an average says little about a typical member. "Majority" means most (more than half).

## N4. Causation + language strength
- Words that signal a causal conclusion (X makes Y happen): causes, leads to, results in, is responsible for, effect of, due to, produces. Example: "Screen time leads to poor sleep."
- Correlation means X and Y show up together. Concluding "X causes Y" only because they occur together is a flaw by default. Example: "Ice cream sales and drownings both rise in summer, so ice cream causes drowning."
- Six ways to weaken a causal claim: (1) an alternative cause (hot weather causes both); (2) reverse causation (Y actually caused X); (3) coincidence or chance; (4) the cause is present but the effect isn't; (5) the effect is present without the cause; (6) a problem with the data (small or biased study).
- Ways to strengthen a causal claim: rule out an alternative cause, rule out reverse causation, show the cause and effect occurring together again, or show that without the cause the effect doesn't happen.
- Causal flaw answers say the argument assumes correlation proves causation, or fails to consider an alternative cause or reverse causation.
- Necessary is not sufficient: "X is needed for Y" does not mean "X guarantees Y" (oxygen is needed for fire, but oxygen alone doesn't start one). "X can produce Y" does not mean "only X produces Y".
- Strong words (claim a lot, hard to prove): must, always, never, all, none, only, proves, certainly, impossible.
- Weak words (claim little, easy to prove): some, can, may, might, sometimes, possible, at least one, not all, could.
- For NA, MBT, MSS, and agreement questions prefer weak, modest answers, since they're easier to prove. For SA, PSA, strengthen, and weaken questions strong answers are fine as long as they are relevant.
- "Can", "may", "sometimes" claims are easy to prove (one case is enough) and hard to disprove (you'd have to show it never happens). Example: "some birds can't fly" is proved by one penguin.

## N5. LR types A: assumption family
- Necessary assumption (NA; stems say "relies on", "requires", "depends on"): something the argument needs; if it's false, the C falls apart. It often works as a defender that blocks an objection, and is usually weakly worded. Example: "Tom will win because he's the fastest" needs "nothing else, like a disqualification, will stop the fastest runner from winning."
- NA negation test: negate the answer choice; if the argument collapses, it's the NA. Negate minimally (the simple opposite, not the extreme): "all" becomes "not all", "some" becomes "none", "most" becomes "half or fewer", "X" becomes "not X".
- Sufficient assumption (SA; stems say "if assumed, the conclusion follows logically", "properly drawn"): an answer that, added to the premises, guarantees the C. Strong wording is fine.
- SA bridge: a term that appears in the C but not in the premises must appear in the answer, which links the premise term to the conclusion term (P term → C term, not the reverse). Example: P "Sam is a lawyer", C "Sam is rich"; answer "all lawyers are rich."
- NA vs SA: an NA is needed but may not be enough on its own. An SA is enough to prove the C but may not be needed.
- Strengthen (stems say "most supports", "most justifies"): the answer makes the C more likely. New information is allowed. Treat every choice as true.
- Weaken (stems say "undermines", "calls into question"): the answer makes the C less likely. New information is allowed. Accept the premises as true and attack the link from P to C instead.
- For strengthen and weaken, aim at the gap between P and C; ignore choices that only support or attack a premise.
- EXCEPT stems: four choices do the job; pick the one that doesn't. That answer may simply be irrelevant; it doesn't have to do the opposite.
- PSA (stems say "which principle most helps justify"): an SA written as a general rule; the rule's sufficient (if) part matches the premises and its necessary (then) part matches the C. Example: P "the bridge is unsafe", C "close it"; rule "any unsafe structure should be closed."
- Principle-apply (stems say "conforms to"): find the scenario where the rule's sufficient (if) part is met, so its necessary (then) result must follow. Seeing only the necessary part triggers nothing (rule "if unsafe, close it": a closed bridge doesn't prove it was unsafe).
- Evaluate (stems say "most useful to know"): use the variance test: answer the question both ways (yes and no, high and low); it's correct if one answer strengthens the C and the other weakens it. If both answers leave the C unchanged, it's wrong.
- Flaw (stems say "vulnerable to criticism"): name the flaw yourself before reading the choices (use N7). The correct choice must describe what the argument actually did, not just a flaw it could have had.

## N6. LR types B: inference, structure, other
- Must be true (MBT; stems say "must be true", "properly inferred"): stay inside the text; the answer must be 100% provable from it. Combine the conditionals and quantifiers given (N2, N3). Prefer conservative, modest answers.
- Most strongly supported (MSS): nearly MBT; the answer should be very likely from the text, worded conservatively, with no outside facts.
- Must be false (MBF; stems say "cannot be true"): the answer directly contradicts the text or something validly inferred from it. Example: text says all members voted; answer "one member didn't vote."
- Main point: use the "therefore" test (put "therefore" before the candidate; if the rest supports it, it's the main C). The answer paraphrases the C with the same strength and scope. It is not a sub-conclusion, a premise, or a counterpoint.
- Role (stems say "plays which role"): name the sentence's job yourself (premise, sub-conclusion, conclusion, counterpoint, background, or example) before reading the choices.
- Method (stems say "proceeds by"): describe how the argument works in general terms before the choices (e.g. "uses an analogy", "rules out an alternative"); every word of the answer must match what happened.
- Parallel reasoning: compare conclusions first: same strength (must vs probably), same type (fact, prediction, or recommendation), conditional or not. Diagram any conditionals. The topic doesn't matter and the sentence order may differ.
- Parallel flaw: name the flaw in the stimulus first, then find the choice that commits the same flaw. Example: stimulus reverses a conditional; the answer must reverse one too.
- Resolve (stems say "explain the discrepancy"): state both facts that seem to clash; the correct answer lets both be true at once. An answer that explains only one side is wrong. Example: "more police, more crime" is resolved by "police were added where crime was already rising."
- Point at issue (stems say "disagree about"): both speakers must state an opinion on the choice, and the opinions must differ. For each choice ask: does X have a view? Does Y have a view? Do the views differ?
- Agreement (stems say "both agree"): both speakers must state an opinion on the choice, and the opinions must be the same.
- Parallel and other long questions: if one takes more than about 2 minutes, guess, flag it, and move on (skip-and-flag), then come back.

## N7. Flaw dictionary
| Flaw | Answer wording |
| --- | --- |
| Correlation → causation | "Infers a causal relationship from a correlation." Two things occurring together is taken as proof one causes the other. |
| Ignores alt cause / reverse | "Fails to consider that Y caused X" or "that some other factor caused both." Example: rich people own yachts, so yachts make you rich. |
| Bad sample | "Generalizes from an unrepresentative sample." Example: polling gym members to conclude most people exercise. |
| Ad hominem | "Attacks the source rather than the argument." Example: "Ignore her tax plan; she was once bankrupt." |
| Circular | "Presupposes what it seeks to prove." The C is restated as a premise. |
| Equivocation | "Uses a key term in two different senses." Example: "fair" meaning just, then meaning average. |
| Part → whole / whole → part | "Infers that a group has a property because its members do" (or the reverse). Example: every player is great, so the team is great. |
| False choice | "Treats two options as the only ones possible" when others exist. |
| Bad authority | "Relies on an authority outside their area of expertise." Example: a famous actor on vaccine safety. |
| Popularity | "Treats the fact that many believe a claim as evidence it is true." |
| Absence of evidence | "Treats a lack of proof that a claim is true as proof that it is false" (or lack of disproof as proof). |
| Reversal (conditional) | "Mistakes a necessary condition for a sufficient one." From A → B concludes B → A. |
| Negation (conditional) | "Treats a sufficient condition as a necessary one." From A → B concludes not A → not B. |
| Percent vs number | "Confuses a proportion with an absolute amount." Example: a higher rate taken to mean more cases. |
| Possible → certain | "Treats something that is possible as if it were certain." Example: "could fail" becomes "will fail." |
| Straw man | "Misrepresents the opponent's position" and then attacks the weaker version. |
| Bad analogy | "Relies on a comparison between relevantly dissimilar things." |
| Unproven ≠ false | "Concludes a claim is false because one argument for it is bad." A weak argument doesn't make its C false. |
| Scope shift | "Draws a conclusion about a different group or term than the premises discuss." Example: P about teens, C about all adults. |
| Ought from is | "Infers what should be done from what is the case." Example: "people have always eaten meat, so they should." |

Stem wording in flaw answers: "takes for granted" or "presumes, without justification" means the flaw is an unstated assumption the argument relies on. "Fails to consider" or "overlooks" means the flaw is ignoring a possibility, and that possibility must actually hurt the argument if true (a harmless overlooked possibility is a wrong answer).

## N8. Elimination + traps
- Pre-phrase (predict the answer in your own words) before reading the choices. Then eliminate wrong choices one by one instead of hunting for the right one.
- Out of scope trap: the answer brings in a new group, term, time period, or comparison the argument never discussed. Example: argument about city buses; answer about trains.
- Extreme-wording red flags: always, never, all, only, must, proves, completely, best. Be suspicious unless it's an SA or PSA question (or a strengthen/weaken), where strong wording is fine.
- Reversed trap: the answer flips the direction of a conditional (A → B turned into B → A) or of cause and effect (says Y causes X).
- Half-right trap: the first half fits, but one wrong word anywhere kills the whole answer.
- True but irrelevant trap: an accurate-sounding statement that doesn't affect the C either way.
- Opposite-direction trap: the answer does the reverse of the task (e.g. strengthens on a weaken question). Watch for it especially on EXCEPT and LEAST stems, where the task itself is reversed.
- Premise-booster trap: the answer only supports a premise (shows the fact is true) instead of strengthening the link from P to C.
- Down to two choices: reread the stem and the C, pick the one that hits the gap you found, and check every word of it against the text.

## N9. Reading Comprehension
- Low-resolution summary: for each paragraph, jot 3-6 words on its purpose (e.g. "P2: critics' objection"). Don't memorize details; look them up when a question asks.
- Track voices: the author vs other people (critics, scientists, "some"). Mark whose view each claim is, since questions often test who believes what.
- The author's own view shows in opinion words: judging adjectives and adverbs ("flawed", "convincingly"), plus words like "however", "surprisingly", "unfortunately".
- Pivot words signal a shift in view or a contrast: but, however, yet, although, despite, nevertheless, while.
- Before starting the questions, know three things: the main point (one line), the author's attitude, and the structure map (what each paragraph does).
- Scope: the answer must cover exactly what the passage covers; reject answers too broad (the whole field) or too narrow (one detail).
- Main point and primary purpose questions: the answer should match your low-resolution summary of the whole passage.
- Detail questions (stems say "according to the passage"): go back, find the exact line, and pick the choice that paraphrases it.
- Inference and "most strongly supported" questions in RC: the answer is one small step beyond the text, provable from a specific line, and conservatively worded.
- Attitude questions: match both the direction (positive or negative) and the strength. Authors are rarely extreme, so "mild skepticism" beats "contempt".
- Tone words: positive = laudatory (praising), approving. Neutral = objective, measured, tentative. Negative = skeptical, critical, dismissive.
- "Why does the author mention X" questions: answer with X's job in your structure map (an example, a contrast, support for a claim, etc.), not what X says.
- Analogy and application questions: pick the choice with the same abstract relationship as the passage, not the same topic. Example: a passage on a cure that worsens the illness matches a fix that creates a bigger problem.
- Comparative passages (two short passages, A and B): map A, map B, then name the relationship: agree, disagree, A is an example of B, A general vs B specific, problem vs solution, or different focus. Questions ask what both passages, only one, or neither discusses.
- If you can't point to a line that proves an answer, don't pick it. Never use outside knowledge.

## N10. Timing, test day, blind review
- Each section is 35 minutes. An LR section has about 25 questions, so about 1 minute 24 seconds per question on average; early questions are usually easier, so go faster there and bank time for the harder ones later.
- Two-pass method: on the first pass answer each question, or at about 2 minutes guess, flag it, and move on; after the first pass, return to the flagged questions with the time left.
- RC timing: an RC section has 4 passages, so about 8-9 minutes per passage including its questions.
- Never leave a blank: there is no penalty for wrong answers. With about 1 minute left, fill in a guess for every unanswered question.
- Treat every section as scored: one section is an unscored experimental section, but you aren't told which.
- Test day checklist: photo ID, the system check done in advance (for the remote test), a clear and quiet room, and eat something at the break.
- The day before: no practice test; at most a light review of your journal; get a full night's sleep.
- Writing sample (LSAT Argumentative Writing, done separately, unscored but sent to schools): state a clear position (thesis), give 2-3 reasons, address a counterargument, and close briefly.
- Blind review step 1: take the section timed; circle any question you weren't sure of; don't check the answers yet.
- Blind review step 2: redo every circled question untimed; write down why the right answer is right and why each wrong answer is wrong.
- Blind review step 3: compare your timed score with your blind review score. Still wrong in blind review = an understanding gap; wrong when timed but right in blind review = a timing gap.
- Blind review step 4: log every miss and every lucky guess in the wrong-answer journal (N11) and redo them 1-2 weeks later.

## N11. Wrong-answer journal
| Field | Write |
| --- | --- |
| PT / S / Q# | PrepTest, section, and question number. Example: PT158 S2 Q14. |
| Type | The question type (N5, N6). Example: NA (necessary assumption). |
| Mine / correct / BR | My timed answer / the correct answer / my blind review answer. Example: C / B / B. |
| C + gap | One line: the conclusion, plus what the premises don't prove about it. |
| Why I picked wrong | What attracted me to the wrong answer (e.g. it repeated words from the passage). |
| Why right is right | The line that proves it, or the negation test result (negated, the argument collapses). |
| Trap (N8) | Which trap caught me. Example: too strong (extreme wording). |
| Rule | A one-line takeaway to apply next time. Example: on NA, negate before choosing. |
| Redo date / result | Redo it 1-2 weeks later; write the date and whether I got it right. |
